\section{Related Papers and Solutions} \label{sec:related-papers-and-solutions}

Before starting designing or implementing the system, preceiving steps were made. This included researching related articles on Google Scholar, searching for similar projects on YouTube and formulating key ideas. These ideas quickly reduced to several questions waiting for answers, like \i{Which algorithms could help in achieving the requirements?}, \i{Which are the optimal steps for image processing in our project, leading us from a raw image to labeled cell images?} and \i{To predict the chess piece from a cell image, should one use classification, pattern matching, object tracking or a completely different approach?}.

The problems this project assesses are not new. There are hundreds, if not thousands of attempts to solve the sub-problems that will be met during the designing and development phases. Aspects to keep in mind are familiarity, complexity, time needed to implement and added value. During my university lectures and laboratories, I had the opportunity to get familiar with some algorithms which are described in the books Pattern Recognition Systems, Laboratory Works \cite{prs_labs} and Image Processing, Laboratory Works \cite{ip_labs}. These resources served as a starting point and will be mentioned multiple times throughout this document.

On top of the books mentioned, multiple papers written in the chess domain were examined. These varied in size and contribution to existing solutions. Some of them were too abstract (lacking implementation ideas) and some were out of the scope of this project. Ideas were gathered from various sources, and three really helpful publications must be mentioned.


% --- A Computer Vision System for Chess Game Tracking --- %

\subsection{A Computer Vision System for Chess Game Tracking}

Being the most influential paper, A Computer Vision System for Chess Game Tracking \cite{acvsystem} presents \i{a real-time system that allows the detection of the moves of a chess game}. As its title suggests, it provides a detailed description of the whole pipeline needed to get from an image to actual move detection. 

In its Introduction section, numerous similar and/or related projects are mentioned. The authors place the camera on the top of the board. They detect the occupancy of cells based on average color information. Rectification, intensity adjustment and chessboard position adjustment are done. They take into account the World Chess Federation (FIDE) requirements for board size and color. 

A block diagram of the whole system describes all the steps as follows:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \b{Chessboard Image Frame} \\
    A 5-megapixel Logitech c310 camera is mounted over the board to get the images.
    
    \item \b{Chessboard Grid Detector} \\
    Built-in MATLAB functions are used to get a grid, from which borders and pivot points are extracted.
    
    \item \b{Geometric Rectification} \\
    Using previously detected corner points, the images are warped into 480x480 pixel dimensions.
    
    \item \b{Chessboard Position Adjustment} \\
    White pieces are positioned to be on the bottom of the board. From the previous step, they could be on any side, depending on the order of detected corners.
    
    \item \b{Automatic Camera Exposure Adjustment} \\
    A custom camera exposure adjustment algorithm is defined which \i{maximizes the average of the color differences between light/dark piece and square}.
    
    \item \b{Intensity Adjustment} \\
    A 5x5 median filter is applied, and saturation is increased.
    
    \item \b{Average Color References} \\
    An ROI (Region of Interest) is used to find out if the cell is free or taken. Reference colors are calculated at the beginning.
    
    \item \b{Move Detection} \\
    Moving frames are defined as frames that have a big color difference, having as reference the previous stable frame. 
    
    \item \b{2D Reconstructed Chessboard State and Move Logs}
    After detecting cell states, the movement is deduced from the current and previous states. Six cases are defined based on the differences. If the movement is valid, it is saved.
\end{enumerate}

The writers also suggest using the LAB color space for better performance. They mention shadows as a negative factor in performance. Overall this paper provided a lot of ideas for the project, although I do not like the movement tracking approach, since there is no guarantee the pieces are the same (and in this approach, only two types of pieces exist: black or white).


% --- Stonewall, Live Analysis of Chess Gameplay --- %

\subsection{Stonewall, Live Analysis of Chess Gameplay}

Another highly influential paper was Stonewall, a system for live analysis of chess gameplay and feedback from a chess engine \cite{stonewall}. This application is a project made by a student named Sam Donald, who studied at the University of Canterbury. I found it through a video on YouTube, which showed quite impressive results.

It shares many ideas with the previously mentioned paper: it detects the outermost corners starting from an empty board, uses K-means clustering for reference color calculation, gets moves based on differences from the previous and current state, and applies rectification. However, there are some new ideas presented as well. One of them is having a \b{side-mounted camera}. This has the drawback of perspective distortion and overlaying pieces but produces much more distinguishable piece images. The author ties the program to the Stockfish chess engine to validate movement and to output a reply. The human plays with white, against the machine's black pieces.

Spatial relationships are used to approximate corner locations that are not directly found. Cropping up/ segmenting the image into cells is mentioned, and the issue of \b{pieces occupying two or three cells} due to the angle of the camera is presented. Internally there is an ASCII representation of the board.

Tracking of pieces is done by imported Python libraries. I like custom implementations, so for me, this is not desirable. A list of all possible moves is iterated at each movement, which might be an overhead. Another drawback of the system highlighted by the developer is the hard-coded \b{pawn promotion}. Every pawn is considered to be promoted to a queen, although following the rules of chess, a pawn might be promoted to a rook, bishop or knight as well. Castling is hard-coded, calibration is not automated and it is slightly complicated, and there is a need to press a key at the end of each move.

All in all, this paper served as a good reference point, to see what other students could achieve within this topic. I like the fact that Sam carefully thought of options to handle faults. There is source code available as well, but it is in Python (not in C++) and I did not look over it, since I wanted to implement it from scratch.


% --- Chess Piece Movement Detection and Tracking --- %

\subsection{Chess Piece Movement Detection and Tracking}

Paper Chess Piece Movement Detection and Tracking, A Vision System Framework for Autonomous Chess Playing Robot \cite{chesspiecemovement} is the most technical of the three mentioned papers. It defines a chess-playing robot as \i{a robot that can see, think, and move the chess piece properly}. There is a whole team behind this project, and the work is divided into three parts: mechanics, engine and vision. The paper cited only details the vision part.

Although it is more technical and dry, it mentions a lot of ideas that came to my mind beforehand. \b{Contour tracing} is used to get the borders of the chessboard. They convert the image to grayscale, apply \b{Gaussian filtering} then feed the image to \b{Canny edge detector} and \b{morphological operations}. They mention the issue of borders (the outer part of chessboards which is not cell). \b{Hough Transformation} is mentioned as well; to deal with multiple lines detected very close to each other, they merge them. Cell corners are derived from the intersections of lines. Stable and unstable frame detection is detailed. Blob detection is applied based on subtraction. Four types of moves are defined: normal moves, capturing moves, en-passant moves, and castling moves.

\section{Existing Commercial Products}

We might think of the project as a product since even if there is no profit involved, we make it for some imaginary clients. Although one might not expect it, there is an actual market for smart chess boards. And if there is a market for something, there are products to fill the need. In this section possible competitors are presented, to see what the market offers, and compare it to our solution.


% --- Square Off --- %

\subsection{Square Off}

As you open their website, the team of Square Off boldly displays that they are the distributors of the \i{World's Smartest AI chessboards}. Their vision is to build \i{the future of games and toys with robotics and AI}. With numerous partners showcased, both in the world of sales and chess, it looks like their product is the go-to solution if somebody wants to experiment with smart chessboards.

They offer two products, the PRO board and the Grand Kingdom Set. The PRO is a rollable e-chessboard selling for €299 (around 1.490 RON as of May 7, 2024). It can be connected to the Square Off App. The chessboard is dotted with small LEDs to guide the user. They promise you that by using this board and their applications, your skills and ELO (chess rating) will rise.

You can play against AI (having 20 difficulty levels), in face-to-face mode or online, since you can connect it to Chess.com or Lichess. The board is connected to power through a USB-C port. They have blogs, tutorials, unboxing videos, and reviews from professional and popular chess players. Something I cannot compete with.


% --- ChessUp --- %

\subsection{ChessUp}

A similar product to Square Off. They define their product as \i{a revolutionary smart chess board with a built-in chess engine and chess instructor}. It has built-in AI, touch sense pieces, and light indicators, checks the legality of movements, allows you to play online on Lichess, and has a mobile app. A different solution for the same thing. Their product costs €339.00 (approximately 1690 RON). They are a gaming startup with the mission of making \i{STEM-focused games more approachable}.

We can say that both for ChessUp and Square Off the advantage we have is that we provide a free, open-source solution. While professional solutions will always surpass student projects, they often deny giving you the possibility to tailor the software and hardware to your needs, stripping away some of your freedom for so-called convenience.


% --- Classical Board --- %

\subsection{Classical Board}

As funny as it might sound, a competitor for our solution is a lack of solution. Chess is an old game, and many players prefer to play it ``the old way''. One of the main beauties of the game is that you can take it outside, anywhere, and disconnect from the world for a moment. While developing our solution, we must take into account that over-engineering might kill the magic of playing chess, making our product useless.

\subsection{A survey on Hough Transform}

The paper A survey on Hough Transform \cite{hough_survey} provides a comprehensive presentation of the Hough algorithm. Its abstract notes: \i{Our survey, along with more than 200 references, will help
the researchers and students to get a comprehensive view on HT and guide them in applying it properly
to their problems of interest}. In the Introduction section, the general idea is presented, after which in the section Formulation different line representations and parametrizations are shown. While the base idea is simple if you get hold of it, there are many variations to achieve better results and to become more time- and space-efficient. Many real-life applications are listed, like medical imaging, motion detection and robot navigation.


% --- K Nearest Neighbors --- %

\subsection{K Nearest Neighbors}

While the KNN algorithm is straightforward and well explained in \cite{prs_labs}, I was curious what others have to say about it. I found the paper K-nearest neighbor from Leif E. Peterson \cite{knn}. He gives a good overview of the algorithm, formalizes ideas, and tries to explain every term he runs into. After the second section, the discussion becomes very generic, issuing topics like building the confusion matrix, standardization and fuzzification, cross-validation and some performance evaluation. It was a good refresher and a reassurance that my ideas were in place.


% --- Support Vector Machine --- %

\subsection{Support Vector Machine}

While trying to get a better understanding of how support vector machines work, I stumbled upon a snippet of IEEE Intelligent Systems and their Applications publication from 1998 \cite{svm}. This resource contained four articles: \i{SVMs - a practical consequence of learning theory}, \i{Using SVMs for text categorization}, \i{Applying SVMs to face detection} and \i{How to implement SVMs}. Each article has its bibliography, and a listing of people contributing.

In the first article, we get a general introduction to SVMs, as this was something experimental at the time. Bernhard Schölkopf, the author, argues that it is worth studying the functioning of an SVM since it is not just ``yet-another-algorithm''. He describes the hyperplane classifiers, feature spaces and kernels, presents the kernel trick, and gives intuitive examples. Developments and open issues are also showcased.

The second article describes the usage of SVM for assigning predefined categories (based on content) to ``unseen'' text, and proudly presents the practical results. A linear SVM is used, and in this case, the weights have an easy-to-understand role in classification. A detailed comparison is made with other algorithms (findism, naive Bayes, Bayes nets, trees), the metric being the average of precision and recall (breakeven point).

The third article uses SVM for detecting squares that contain faces. What stood out to me was that it is a binomial classification (face and non-face), and that a big part of the success of the experimentation is said to come from training on images containing 0 faces and putting every false positive into the training set. They experiment on real-time systems, but you can imagine what can be done with a Windows NT Pentium 200-MHz computer.

The last article tries to demystify the implementation of SVMs, however, it quickly became too technical for me, since it insists a lot on the different solutions for solving the QP (quadratic programming) problem, and discusses optimizations rather than the base algorithm. Overall it is nice to see that these algorithms have been around for so long and that the theory was developed early, sometimes the only thing holding back being the lack of computational power.
