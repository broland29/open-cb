\section{Related Research Work}

Before starting implementation or design, I did research on the internet (mainly on Google Scholar and YouTube) about projects in the domain. The main questions I wanted to get answers for were: \textit{What algorithms should I apply?} \textit{What would be the steps of getting cell images from raw images?} and \textit{Should I perform classification, pattern matching, object tracking or use other methods?}.

Before any research, all I had was a bunch of ideas and algorithms I learned at university. These can be found in the books Pattern Recognition Systems, Laboratory Works \cite{prs_labs} and Image Processing, Laboratory Works \cite{ip_labs}, and will be mentioned multiple times throughout this document. I looked through multiple papers, since there are a lot of projects in the chess domain, varying in size and contribution to existing solutions. Most papers were either too abstract (lacking implementation ideas; most of them have no code at all), brought nothing new to the table, or were ``out of my league''. However, ideas were gathered from various sources, and I must mention three really helpful publications.


\subsection{A Computer Vision System for Chess Game Tracking}

Probably the most influential paper for me was A Computer Vision System for Chess Game Tracking \cite{acvsystem} presents `` a real-time system that allows the detection of the moves of a chess game''. As its title suggests, it provides a detailed description of the whole pipeline needed to get from an image to actual move detection. 

In the Introduction section, numerous similar and/or related projects are mentioned. The authors place the camera on the top of the board. They detect the occupancy of cells based on average color information. Rectification, intensity adjustment and chessboard position adjustment are done. They take into account the World Chess Federation (FIDE) requirements for board size and color. 

A block diagram of the whole system describes all the steps as follows:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Chessboard Image Frame

    A 5-megapixel Logitech c310 camera is mounted over the board to get the images.
    
    \item Chessboard Grid Detector

    Built-in MATLAB functions are used to get a grid, from which borders and pivot points are extracted.
    
    \item Geometric Rectification

    Using previously detected corner points, the images are warped into 480x480 pixel dimensions.
    
    \item Chessboard Position Adjustment

    White pieces are positioned to be on the bottom of the board. From the previous step, they could be on any side, depending on the order of detected corners.
    
    \item Automatic Camera Exposure Adjustment

    A custom camera exposure adjustment algorithm is defined which ``maximizes the average of the color differences between light/dark piece and square''.
    
    \item Intensity Adjustment

    A 5x5 median filter is applied, and saturation is increased.
    
    \item Average Color References

    An ROI (Region of Interest) is used to find out if the cell is free or taken. Reference colors are calculated at the beginning.
    
    \item Move Detection

    Moving frames are defined as frames that have a big color difference, having as reference the previous stable frame. 
    
    \item 2D Reconstructed Chessboard State and Move Logs

    After detecting cell states, the movement is deduced from the current and previous states. Six cases are defined based on the differences. If the movement is valid, it is saved.
\end{enumerate}

The writers also suggest using the LAB color space for better performance. They mention shadows as a negative factor in performance. Overall this paper provided a lot of ideas for this project, although I do not like the movement tracking approach, since there is no guarantee the pieces are the same (and in this approach, only two types of pieces exist: black or white).



\subsection{Stonewall, Live Analysis of Chess Gameplay}

Another highly influential paper was Stonewall, a system for live analysis of chess gameplay and feedback from a chess engine \cite{stonewall}. This application is a project of a student named Sam Donald, who studied at the University of Canterbury. I found it through a video on YouTube, which showed quite impressive results.

It shares many ideas with the previously mentioned paper: it detects the outermost corners starting from an empty board, uses K-means clustering for reference color calculation, gets move based on differences from the previous and current state, applies rectification. However, there are some new ideas presented as well. One of them is having a side-mounted camera. This has the drawback of perspective distortion and overlaying pieces but produces much more distinguishable piece images. The author ties the program to the Stockfish chess engine to validate movement and to output a reply. The human plays with white, against the machine's black pieces.

Spatial relationships are used to approximate corner locations that are not directly found. Cropping up/ segmenting the image into cells is mentioned, and the issue of pieces occupying two or three cells due to the angle of the camera is presented. Internally there is an ASCII representation of the board.

Tracking of pieces is done by imported Python libraries. I like custom implementations, so for me, this is not desirable. A list of all possible moves is iterated at each movement, which might be an overhead. Another drawback of the system highlighted by the developer is the hard-coded pawn promotion (every pawn is considered to be promoted to a queen, although following the rules of chess, a pawn might be promoted to a rook, bishop or knight as well), hard-coded casting, non-automated and slightly complicated calibration and the need of pressing a key at the end of each move.

All in all, this paper served as a reference point, to see what other students could achieve within this topic. I like the fact that Sam carefully thought of options to handle faults. There is source code available as well, but it is in Python (not in C++) and I did not look over it, since I wanted to implement from scratch.




\subsection{Chess Piece Movement Detection and Tracking}

Paper Chess Piece Movement Detection and Tracking, A Vision System Framework for Autonomous Chess Playing Robot \cite{chesspiecemovement} is the most technical of the three mentioned papers. It defines a chess-playing robot as a robot that can see, think, and move the chess piece properly. There is a whole team behind this project, and the work is divided into three parts: mechanics, engine and vision. The paper cited only details the vision part.


Although it is more technical and dry, it mentions a lot of ideas that came to my mind beforehand. Contour tracing is used to get the borders of the chessboard. They convert the image to grayscale, apply Gaussian filtering then feed the image to Canny edge detection and morphological operations. They mention the issue of borders (the outer part of chessboards which is not cell). Hough Transformation is mentioned as well; to deal with multiple lines detected very close to each other, they merge them. Cell corners are derived from the intersections of lines. Stable and unstable frame detection is detailed. Blob detection is applied based on subtraction. Four types of moves are defined: ``normal moves, capturing moves, en-passant moves, and castling moves''.



\section{Existing Products}

Although you might not expect it, there is an actual market for smart chess boards. And if there is a market for something, there are products to fill the need. In this section, I will showcase two professional solutions for (parts of) what I intend to implement.

\subsection{Square Off}

As you enter their website, the team of Square Off boldly displays that they have the ``World's Smartest A.I. chessboards''. Their vision is to build ``the future of games and toys with robotics and AI''. With numerous partners showcased, both in the world of sales and chess, it looks like their product is the go-to if somebody wants to experiment with smart chessboards.

They offer two products, the PRO board and the Grand Kingdom Set. The PRO is a rollable e-chessboard selling for €299 (around 1.490 RON as of May 7, 2024), It can be connected to the Square Off App. The chessboard is dotted with small LEDs to guide the user. They promise you that by using this board and their applications, your skills and ELO will rise.

You can play against AI (having 20 difficulty levels), in face-to-face mode or online, since you can connect it to Chess.com or Lichess. The board is connected to power through a USB-C port. They have blogs, tutorials, unboxing videos, and reviews from professional and popular chess players.

\subsection{ChessUp}

They define their product as ``a revolutionary smart chess board with a built-in chess engine and chess instructor''. It has built-in AI, touch sense pieces, and light indicators, checks the legality of movements, allows you to play online on Lichess, and has a mobile app. A different solution for the same thing. Their product costs €339.00 (approximately 1690 RON). They are a gaming startup with the mission of making ``STEM-focused games more approachable''.

\subsection{Classical Board}

As funny as it might sound, a competitor for our solution is a lack of solution. Chess is an old game, and many players prefer to play it ``the old way''. One of the main beauties of the game is that you can take it outside or anywhere and disconnect from the world for a moment. While developing our solution, we must take into account that over-engineering might kill the magic of playing chess, making our product useless.
